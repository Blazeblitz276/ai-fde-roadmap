FDE craft: technical demos and one-page design docs

Track: FDE craft. Builds on drop 6 (customer discovery, scoping a pilot) and drop 7 (SLOs).
After discovery and scoping, two artefacts move a deal and a project forward: a demo that proves value on the customer's own problem, and a short design doc that gets their architects, security and business owners to say yes to the same plan. Both are communication tools, not engineering showcases.

What it is and the problem it solves

ArtefactAudienceProblem it solvesGood outcome
Technical demoBusiness sponsor, end users, sometimes executivesSlides and promises do not convince; people need to see their own workflow get easier'Can we have this for my team next month?' plus agreed success metrics
One-page design docCustomer architects, security, IT ops, your own teamEveryone imagines a different system; risks surface late and expensivelySigned-off scope, architecture, non-goals, risks and owners before building

How it works

  1. Demo: start from the painOpen with the user's current workflow and its cost ('this takes Priya 3 hours every Monday'). Show the same task done with your system on their real or realistic data, ideally driven by someone from their team.
  2. Demo: one path, rehearsed, with a fallbackShow one end-to-end story, not every feature. Rehearse on the actual network and accounts; keep a recorded backup and cached results in case the live model or Wi-Fi fails. Show one failure handled gracefully (the agent asks for approval, refuses, or cites sources): it builds more trust than perfection.
  3. Demo: end with a decisionClose with the success metric and the next step (pilot scope, data access, dates), not 'any questions?'.
  4. Doc: one page, decision-focusedContext, goals and non-goals, the proposed design (one diagram), alternatives considered and why not, risks and mitigations (security, data, cost, SLOs), open questions with owners, and the success metric. Trade-offs are the point; implementation detail is not.
  5. Doc: review and keep it aliveCirculate before the meeting, collect comments, record decisions in the doc, and update it when the design changes so it becomes the project's memory.
Everyday comparison: a wedding caterer's tasting and the signed menu.
The demo is the tasting session: the family tries a few real dishes made for their event, not a brochure of everything the caterer can cook. The one-page design doc is the signed menu sheet afterwards: dishes, quantities, what is not included (non-goals), allergies (risks), who confirms the hall timings (owners). Skip the tasting and nobody is excited; skip the menu sheet and everyone remembers a different promise on the wedding day.

Pattern card: demo on their data, then a one-page decision doc

What it is
Prove value with a short, rehearsed demo of one real workflow on the customer's data, then capture scope, architecture, non-goals and risks in a one-page doc that their architects and security sign off.
Metaphor
Caterer's tasting session, then the signed menu sheet.
Real example
Palantir's AIP Bootcamps: customers build a working use case on their own data in one to five days, replacing pilots that took one to three months (Palantir blog, Q3 2023 earnings call). Google's design docs: informal docs focused on trade-offs, goals and non-goals, with 1-3 page 'mini design docs' for smaller work (Malte Ubl, Design Docs at Google).
Use when
Any customer pilot or architecture choice that touches their data, cloud, identity or budget.
Do not use when
Tiny, unambiguous changes; internal throwaway prototypes.
Trade-offs
A few days of preparation and review in exchange for faster yes decisions and fewer late surprises.
Instruction to AI / a teammate
Draft a one-page design doc for the claims-triage pilot with context, goals, non-goals, one architecture diagram, two alternatives we rejected and why, top five risks with mitigations, open questions with owners, and the success metric (triage time from 2 days to 4 hours), written for the customer's security and architecture reviewers.

Real-world scenarios: Palantir AIP Bootcamps and Google design docs

Palantir's blog post on how AIP Bootcamps work describes hands-on sessions where participants go from zero to a use case in one to five days on their own data, rather than watching pre-baked demos. On its Q3 2023 earnings call, Palantir said it had reoriented its go-to-market around bootcamps that deliver real workflows on actual customer data in five days or less, versus traditional pilots that generally took one to three months, and was on track for bootcamps with more than 140 organisations by the end of that November. This is the demo principle at its strongest: the customer's own team builds on their own data.

Malte Ubl's \"Design Docs at Google\" (2020) describes design docs as relatively informal documents written before coding that record the high-level strategy and key decisions, with emphasis on trade-offs. Their purposes include catching design issues while changes are still cheap, reaching consensus, making sure cross-cutting concerns such as security and privacy are covered, and forming the organisation's memory of why decisions were made. It also warns that writing them is overhead: if the solution is not ambiguous, skip the doc.

Why it matters on the job
FDE and architect roles are judged as much on moving decisions as on building. A demo that lands and a one-page doc that their security team approves in one review is what turns a pilot into production, and in interviews these are the stories that show you can lead.

FDE / architect interview angle

  • Tell me about a demo that went wrong, or how you would prepare one for a sceptical CTO. Strong answer: start from their pain and metric, one rehearsed path on their data, someone from their team driving, a fallback recording, one failure handled visibly, and a clear ask at the end (pilot scope, data access, date).
  • Walk me through how you get a customer's architects and security team aligned before building. Strong answer: a one-page design doc with goals and non-goals, a diagram, alternatives and trade-offs, risks (data, network, identity, cost, SLOs) and owners, circulated before the review, decisions recorded, kept updated.

Glossary

FDE
Forward-deployed engineer: an engineer embedded with customers to build and deploy solutions on their problems.
Pilot / proof of value
A limited real deployment to prove the solution works and is worth paying for.
Stakeholder
Anyone affected by or able to block the project (sponsor, users, IT, security, legal).
Design doc
A short document describing a proposed design and its trade-offs before building.
Goals / non-goals
What the project will achieve / what it explicitly will not do.
Cross-cutting concerns
Things that affect the whole system: security, privacy, cost, reliability.
ADR
Architecture Decision Record: a short note recording one decision and why.
Success metric
The measurable result that decides if the pilot worked.
Happy path
The scenario where everything goes right.
Feature flag
A switch that turns behaviour on or off without redeploying.
Preview deployment
An automatic temporary copy of the app for one branch or change.
Seed data / masked data
Sample data loaded for testing / real data with sensitive fields hidden.
AIP
Palantir's Artificial Intelligence Platform.
Go-to-market
How a company sells and delivers its product to customers.
