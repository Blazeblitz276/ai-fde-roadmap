FDE craft: customer discovery and scoping a pilot from MVP to production

Track: FDE craft. This is the non-technical core of the FDE job, and the part interviewers probe hardest.
A forward deployed engineer (FDE) sits with a customer, finds the problem worth solving, builds something that works on their real data, and gets it into production. The hard part is rarely the code (agentic tools write much of it). It is choosing the right problem, defining what 'working' means as a number, and avoiding the classic trap: a great demo that never reaches production.

What it is and the problem it solves

TermOne-line ideaProblem it solves
Customer discoveryStructured conversations and shadowing to learn the customer's workflow, pain, data and constraints before buildingBuilding the wrong thing; solving the stated request instead of the real pain
ScopingTurning a big ambition ('AI for our support team') into one narrow use case with a measurable outcome, a deadline and a list of what is out of scopeEndless pilots that try to do everything and prove nothing
PilotA time-boxed (often 6-12 week) real deployment with real users and real data, judged against agreed success numbers'Pilot purgatory': demos that impress but never earn a production budget
MVP to productionThe staged path from prototype to a system with security, monitoring, evals, cost limits and an ownerPrototypes that break on real data volume, permissions or edge cases

The phrase OpenAI's FDE lead uses is the 'deployment gap': the distance between what models appear capable of and what people can reliably use in their work. Closing that gap, one customer at a time, is the job.

How it works

  1. Discover: ask about the work, not the AIWho does this task today, how often, how long does it take, what does a mistake cost, where does the data live, who can say yes to production (budget owner), who will say no (security, legal, works council)? Shadow a real user for an hour: you will learn more than in a week of meetings.
  2. Score candidate use casesFor each idea rate value (time or money saved), feasibility (is the data available and clean enough, can today's model do it), and risk (what happens when it is wrong). Pick high value + feasible + tolerable risk. Write a one-page scope: problem, users, success metric, data sources, out of scope, timeline.
  3. Define 'good' before buildingSit with the customer's experts and build a golden set of real examples with correct answers (drop 3 evals). Agree the bar: e.g. '85% of tickets correctly categorised, under 3 seconds, under 2 cents each'. This turns 'it feels good' into a number nobody can argue with.
  4. MVP: thinnest end-to-end sliceReal data in, real output out, ugly UI is fine. Measure against the golden set from day one. Show progress to the customer every week.
  5. Pilot with real usersA small group uses it in their daily work, with a human checking outputs. Track the agreed metric, adoption (do they actually use it?) and failure examples, which feed back into the golden set.
  6. Gate, then productioniseIf the metric is met: harden (IAM drop 1, monitoring drop 4, cost budgets drop 3, guardrails topic 3 below, CI/CD topic 4 below), name an owner on the customer side, document and hand over. If not met: rescope rather than drift.
Everyday comparison: a good family doctor.
A patient walks in saying 'I need antibiotics' (the customer saying 'we need a chatbot'). A good doctor asks about symptoms, history and lifestyle (discovery), picks the one problem to treat first (scope), agrees how you will know it worked, like a blood test number in four weeks (success metric), tries a treatment for a fixed period (pilot), and only then commits to a long-term plan (production). Prescribing whatever the patient asked for is the 'impressive demo' trap.

When to use / when not to use

Solid arrows = yes, dashed = no.

SignalGood pilot candidateBad pilot candidate
VolumeDone hundreds of times a weekDone twice a year
Cost of errorA human reviews; mistakes are cheap to catchOne mistake is a lawsuit or a safety incident
DataExists, you can get access in daysLocked in a system needing 6 months of approvals
SponsorA named business owner who wants it'Innovation team' with no budget owner
MetricAlready measured today (handle time, backlog)Nobody knows the current baseline

Trade-offs: a narrow scope is less exciting to executives but far more likely to reach production; a broader 'platform' pilot impresses but usually stalls. Saying 'the model cannot do this yet' early costs you a demo but earns trust.

Pattern card: outcome-first pilot

What it is
Agree the business outcome and its measurement before building, prove it on one narrow use case with real users, then scale what worked.
Metaphor
The family doctor: diagnose, treat one thing, check the blood test, then plan long term.
Real example
OpenAI's FDE team (see scenario below): 'Start with the outcome, do what it takes to succeed, then follow with scale.' Palantir FDSEs run time-boxed pilots that start with 'what problem should we tackle in 90 days'.
Use when
Any new customer engagement, internal AI initiative, or when a stakeholder says 'we want AI'.
Do not use when
The task is a routine, well-understood integration with a fixed spec; then just deliver it.
Trade-offs
Slower start (1-2 weeks of discovery before code); far fewer dead-end projects.
Instruction to AI / a teammate
Before writing any code, draft a one-page scope for the invoice-matching use case with the user group, the current baseline, the success metric (target: 90% correct matches on a 200-example golden set from the finance team), data sources and access owners, an out-of-scope list, and a 8-week pilot plan with a go/no-go date.

Real-world scenario and why it matters on the job

OpenAI (Colin Jarvis, Head of Forward Deployed Engineering, 'What FDE means at OpenAI', 22 Oct 2026). In mid-2025 around 15 FDEs worked with a large semiconductor customer on 10+ use cases (test generation, root-cause debugging, an architecture assistant). FDEs who had never worked in chips embedded with the verification team, learned the domain, and built evals with the customer's engineers to test whether the AI could really generate useful tests and diagnose failures. Within 3-6 months all use cases reached production, with about 48% time saved on directed test generation. In another engagement, a telecom voice agent scored about 60% on the key evals during scoping; OpenAI told the customer honestly, brought in Research, and over six months the evals rose to 90%+, the level needed for production. With Thrive Holdings, a Tax AI pilot processed 7,000 tax returns and raised throughput by about 50%.

Palantir: a Palantir forward deployed software engineer describes a three-month pilot with a retail energy provider where the first job was deciding 'what problem we should tackle in 90 days', by interviewing the client and reading up on the energy market before building (Built In NYC, 2018). Palantir's own FDSE blog lists the daily mix: designing workflows, data pipelines, access controls for regulatory needs, and handling production outages with the customer.

Why it matters: as a Lead FDE you will be judged on outcomes reaching production, not on demos. The pattern is the same in both companies: learn the domain, make evals with the customer's experts, agree numbers, be honest about model limits. A computer-vision background is an asset here: inspection and defect-detection pilots have very clear metrics (precision, recall, false reject rate) that customers already track.

Where it shows up in code you'll read

# Python, conceptually: the scope becomes a config + an eval gate in the repo
SCOPE = {
    "use_case": "invoice_matching",
    "metric": "match_accuracy",
    "target": 0.90,          # agreed with finance team
    "max_cost_per_item": 0.02,
    "golden_set": "evals/invoices_200.jsonl",
}

score = run_eval(pipeline, SCOPE["golden_set"])
if score >= SCOPE["target"]:
    print("GO: ready for pilot users")
else:
    print("NO-GO: rescope or add human review")
// TypeScript / Next.js: the same gate often runs in CI (topic 4) via an eval script
//   e.g. 'npm run eval' fails the build if accuracy < target.
// Java / Spring: a JUnit test that loads the golden set and asserts accuracy >= 0.90.
// The point: the agreed success number lives in the code, so nobody can quietly lower it.

FDE / architect interview angle

  • 'A customer says: we want an AI agent for our operations team. What do you do in week one?' Strong answer: discovery, not code. Meet users and the budget owner, shadow the workflow, list candidate tasks, score them on value / feasibility / risk, pick one, agree a measurable target and a golden set with their experts, and set a pilot end date with a go/no-go decision.
  • 'Tell me about a pilot that did not go to production.' Strong answer covers why (no owner, no data access, metric not met), what you learned, and how you now de-risk earlier: data access check in week one, baseline measured before building, a spike against a small golden set before committing.

Glossary

FDE / FDSE
Forward deployed (software) engineer: an engineer embedded with a customer to build and deploy solutions on their real problems.
Deployment gap
OpenAI's term for the distance between what a model seems able to do and what people can reliably use in production.
Discovery
Learning the customer's workflow, pain, data and decision-makers before proposing a solution.
Shadowing
Watching a real user do the task in their normal work.
Scope / out of scope
What the project will deliver, and the explicit list of what it will not.
MVP
Minimum viable product: the smallest version that delivers real value end to end.
Pilot
A time-boxed trial with real users and data, judged against agreed metrics.
Pilot purgatory
When pilots keep running or get repeated but never become production systems.
Baseline
How the task performs today without the new system; what you compare against.
Golden set / eval
Fixed real examples with correct answers, used to measure quality (drop 3).
Go / no-go gate
A planned decision point: continue to production, rescope, or stop.
Human in the loop
A person reviews or approves AI output before it takes effect.
Budget owner / sponsor
The person who can approve money and production rollout.
Precision / recall
Of what the system flagged, how much was right / of what it should have flagged, how much it caught.
CI
Continuous integration: automatic checks on every code change (topic 4).
